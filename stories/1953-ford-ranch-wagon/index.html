A true story about a family and their 1953 Ford Ranch Wagon

Coming from Facebook? Skip to where you left off

When a 1953 Ford Ranch Wagon went up for auction in 2024, the seller included something most used cars had lost long ago.

A canceled check from February 1953.

It came from Chicoine Motor Company in Chadron, Nebraska, where the seller's grandfather had purchased the station wagon brand new.

There was also the original title, the first insurance policy, and a book called Ford at Fifty.

For seventy-one years, the family had kept far more than the car.

They had kept its paper trail, too.

In 1953, a two-door Ranch Wagon was practical family transportation. Its long roof, roomy rear compartment, and straightforward V8 engine made it useful for errands, luggage, and whatever a household needed to carry.

This one left the factory painted Raven Black. Later it acquired a contrasting yellow roof, giving it the cheerful appearance seen in the auction photographs.

The years were not always gentle.

At some point, the wagon struck a garage wall. Its passenger side and tailgate were damaged, leaving work for a future owner.

Still, the family held on.

In 1980, the original purchaser gave the Ford to a grandchild.

The wagon had now passed from the person who signed that 1953 check to another generation who understood why it mattered.

For years it remained in the family, even when old-car ownership meant dealing with worn parts and repairs that couldn't be solved at a modern dealership.

Between 2009 and 2012, the Ford received a substantial refurbishment, and the old wagon became presentable again.

Yet its 239-cubic-inch V8 and three-speed manual transmission with overdrive remained part of the experience.

Then came a harder decision.

In August 2024, after more than seven decades, the family offered the Ranch Wagon at auction with no reserve.

The car came with refurbishment photographs, two spare wheels and tires, Ford literature, and other memorabilia. Most revealing were those surviving papers from its first days in Nebraska.

Fourteen bids later, the Ford sold for $9,000.

Its future belonged to someone outside the family for the first time since it was new.

The auction listing could tell a buyer how the car ran and where the paint needed attention. It couldn't tell every family story that had taken place inside.

But the papers gave a glimpse of one.

Someone had been proud enough of a new Ford in 1953 to save the check that paid for it.

And generations later, that small piece of paper was still traveling with the car.
