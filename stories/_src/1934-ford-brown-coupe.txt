When Eli English was eight years old, he spotted something through the open door of a garage in New Hampshire.
A purple 1934 Ford coupe.
He saw it from the school bus, parked in the shadows, and he never forgot it.
Eli grew up around old Fords, but that low-slung hot rod had a special hold on his imagination. It was one reason he eventually made restoring vintage cars his profession.
For years, he wondered what had happened to the purple coupe.
Then, roughly thirty years later, a friend mentioned a local woman named Ruth Brown who had some old equipment to sell.
Eli realized her address was near the garage he remembered.
He went to see her.
And there, beneath dust, boxes, and plywood, was the exact same Ford.
Ruth explained that her husband John had bought the hot rod in 1963 and driven it for about three years.
He later took it apart for modifications, and the car was parked in 1966. In the 1980s, John and their son Jeff tried to restart the project, but it remained unfinished.
Over the years, people had made offers for the Ford. Ruth refused to let it go cheaply.
The coupe was part of her family's history.
Eli proposed something different: preserve the car and make it safe to drive again, using as many original parts as possible.
Ruth agreed.
The work began in late 2014. Eli searched the garage, barn, and attic for missing pieces. Purple overspray helped identify the original wooden floor. Even the old upholstered seats survived, although they were full of acorns.
An original 1964 photograph of John sitting in the coupe guided the restoration.
But the family received heartbreaking news before it was finished.
~~~
John died from complications of Alzheimer's disease.
He never got to see his hot rod return to the road.
Eli and his crew continued their careful work. They replaced parts necessary for safety while preserving the car's old character and unmistakable purple paint.
On April 16, 2016, the Ford moved under its own power again after roughly fifty years.
Ruth was there. So were her son Jeff and grandson Tanner.
For Jeff, it was an especially remarkable day: he had helped his father work on the car decades earlier, but had never driven it.
Now he finally could.
The little boy who spotted the Ford from a school bus had grown into the craftsman who saved it.
And the family that refused to sell had received something better than a buyer's check.
They had their father's old hot rod back on the road.
John wasn't there to take the ride, but three generations of Browns could finally share the car he had loved.
