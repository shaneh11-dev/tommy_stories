In 1978, Glen Andrews heard about a truck in North Carolina that almost nobody could see.
Household clutter and forgotten objects had been piled in and around it for years. Beneath them sat a worn-out 1938 Chevrolet half-ton pickup.
The truck had once worked hard for a water-well digging company. It was bought new by the father of Glen's father-in-law, and for twelve years it hauled heavy equipment across local roads.
After a long working life and too many expensive problems, it had been retired.
The old Chevrolet was pushed into a one-car shed. More things were stacked on top of it. Then more years passed.
When the original owner's son, Glen's father-in-law, told him about the truck, it had been sitting there for roughly twenty-eight years.
Glen managed to get close enough to examine the cab and engine compartment. He made a promise to his father-in-law: one day, he would bring the Chevy back to life.
It was an easy promise to make. Keeping it would be much harder.
Eight years passed before Glen finally bought the truck in 1986.
By then, it was a major project. He disassembled much of it, but work and family moves interrupted his plans. The pieces traveled with him through two job transfers.
At times, the project must have looked much like the original truck in the shed: something waiting for the right season of life.
In June 1994, Glen finally began the restoration in earnest. The work continued as he moved from Raleigh, North Carolina, to Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.
Four years went into bringing it back.
And Glen had a particular goal. He didn't want to make an old truck merely attractive. He wanted it to look as it had when it left the factory.
~~~
That meant sweating the smallest details.
The body returned to Brewster Green with black fenders and running boards. A specialist recreated the delicate factory pinstripes using photographs Glen had taken before removing the old paint.
He kept the details that modern restorers often change: a single taillight, one windshield wiper, and a simple cab without luxuries like a heater.
The finished Chevrolet was a far cry from the workhorse that had disappeared beneath household junk.
Glen kept improving his research and correcting small details as new information surfaced. He joined vintage vehicle clubs and took the pickup to shows, where judges appreciated the care he had taken.
In June 2019, the Antique Automobile Club of America awarded the Chevrolet a Grand National honor in Auburn, Indiana.
The truck that once hauled well-digging equipment had become an award-winning piece of family and local history.
But the award was not where its story began.
It began with a young man squeezing past clutter in a shed and telling his father-in-law he would save an old family truck.
Decades later, his promise was still rolling down the road.
