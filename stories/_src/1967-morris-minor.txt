A grandmother bought a new Morris Minor in 1967, never imagining it would one day carry her great-grandchild home from the hospital.
And that still was not the end of the car's family story.
In September 1967, Amy Hoar paid £628 for a brand-new two-door Morris Minor in England. She used it for nearly a decade, until failing eyesight and health made driving difficult.
By then, her grandson Greg was a teenager, eager to get behind the wheel.
Amy let him learn in her little car. In 1976, Greg passed his driving test in the very Morris his grandmother had purchased.
He kept borrowing it. Eventually, the arrangement became permanent.
The Minor, affectionately called Moggie, was not fast or fashionable. But Greg trusted it. It carried him to school and along quiet country lanes, and it turned up in family photographs for years.
Then the car began participating in a different part of his life.
Greg met Nicky, who would become his wife. She learned to drive in Moggie, too. The two married, and the little Morris remained in the family.
In 1986, their daughter was born. Greg and Nicky brought her home from Poole Maternity Hospital in the same car that once belonged to great-grandmother Amy.
As their two children grew, both learned to drive in it, too.
Every new milestone added another memory to the old car. But time was taking its toll on the body and mechanical parts.
After decades of use, Greg faced a choice familiar to anyone who owns an aging automobile: replace it, or find a way to keep it going.
~~~
He chose the Morris.
Greg kept it going with a refresh about every ten years, including a thorough rebuild for its fiftieth birthday in 2017.
By 2023, Moggie was 56 years old and still on the road with Greg and Nicky in Dorset.
That year, reporters asked Greg why he kept such an old vehicle on the road. The answer was not about its market value.
It was about Amy, the grandmother who first bought it. About learning to drive. About courting Nicky. About the day they carried their baby daughter home.
Now there was another young family member taking notice. Greg's grandson Joseph, fourteen at the time, already had his eye on the Morris for when he became old enough to drive.
The little Morris had started as a grandmother's runabout. More than half a century later, it was still teaching her descendants how to travel through life.
A grandmother could hardly have imagined a more lasting gift.
