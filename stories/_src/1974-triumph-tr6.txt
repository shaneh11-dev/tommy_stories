As a teenager, Tami Biase had an unusual answer when people asked how she wanted to spend her summer.
She wanted to sand a rusty old Triumph.
Tami's father had spent years restoring classic vehicles. He could identify passing cars almost instantly, and he seemed capable of fixing nearly anything.
To Tami, her dad was a sort of superhero. She liked accompanying him to car shows and watching him work in the family's garage.
Then she spotted a bright yellow Triumph TR6 and fell for its wide tires, low shape, and wooden dashboard.
She began saving money from her jobs. As she neared sixteen, she and her father searched the classifieds for a TR6 that needed work.
They found a 1974-and-a-half model in a neighboring town. Its owner was being transferred to Texas and wanted $2,500. The car had dents and rust, so Tami reluctantly walked away.
Then the seller called back. He needed to leave soon and was ready to negotiate.
Tami bought the Triumph for $1,500.
She had her dream car, though not yet a car she could proudly drive.
Her dad had restored a 1937 Ford from the ground up, and the TR6 needed an enormous amount of attention, too. So Tami went through school and then college while spending summers working on the little British convertible.
Instead of long vacations, she chose tools, sandpaper, paint remover, and time in the garage with her father.
Their plans even attracted complaints from a neighbor who disliked the sound of power tools. The two kept working, weekend after weekend.
The project stretched through four summers.
~~~
At last the tired brown Triumph emerged wearing brilliant Porsche Guards Red paint.
Tami had been involved in the details herself. She remembered spending two hours just masking the trim around the windshield before painting.
But looking back, she said the best part was never the finished bodywork.
It was the time with her dad and the skills he had taught her.
Decades later, Tami was still driving the TR6. She wrote about the car for Moss Motoring after more than thirty years of ownership.
The top could go down, the exhaust still sounded wonderful, and the old Triumph continued to demand the attention of someone who knew its peculiarities.
And when it needed fixing, her father was still there to help.
Tami had bought a project car in her teens. Without fully realizing it, she had also found a way to carry her relationship with her father into adulthood.
Some people remember summers by the places they traveled.
Tami remembered hers by the things she and her dad built together, one rusty Triumph part at a time.
