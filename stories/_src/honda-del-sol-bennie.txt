At a car event in Venray, in the Netherlands, one Honda del Sol kept drawing double takes.
People weren't looking under the hood.
They were looking behind the seats.
Because that's where the engine was.
The unusual Honda belonged to a young man named Bennie Kerkhof, and his story had almost nothing to do with showing off.
When Bennie bought the del Sol, it was a conventional front-engine, front-wheel-drive sports car. Like many young enthusiasts, he began making changes.
His older brother had been modifying cars for years and became a trusted source of advice.
Bennie studied automotive engineering and learned to fabricate parts himself. He added a turbocharger, upgraded the brakes, and spent time driving on Germany's Nürburgring.
Then he saw an unfinished project online: a del Sol that someone was converting to rear-wheel drive with its engine behind the seats.
Most people would have dismissed the idea as too complicated.
Bennie wanted to try.
He bought the front structure of a Honda Civic, cut away what he didn't need, and began fitting the suspension and engine mounting points into the back of his del Sol.
The project required endless measurement, cutting, welding, and problem-solving.
But during those months, Bennie was facing something far harder than a mechanical challenge.
His father was in the hospital.
Bennie planned the conversion and worked on the body while his dad was in the hospital. The garage became a place where he could focus on the next small task instead of the fear and sadness surrounding him.
Then his father died.
~~~
Bennie kept working.
He completed nearly all the fabrication himself, asking for help only with a few welding jobs. His mother made room for the project in her garage, and his brother continued to offer guidance.
As the car neared completion, there was one important job left.
Paint.
Bennie found an old spray gun that had belonged to his father. His dad had once used it to paint cars.
Bennie cleaned the gun and decided to use it on the del Sol.
It worked.
When the completed Honda appeared in a 2015 feature, the writer admired the engineering: a front-wheel-drive Honda transformed into a turbocharged, mid-engine machine.
But the detail that mattered most wasn't under the hood or in the trunk.
It was the paint applied with a father's old tool.
Bennie had built something astonishing from an ordinary Honda, but he had also found a way to carry a piece of his dad into the finished project.
The spray gun had once been his father's.
Now the car wearing its paint was his son's.
